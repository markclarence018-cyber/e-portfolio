The story so far

From curiosity to electronics.

A lifelong habit of asking what makes things move, light up, and work.

When I was five years old, I was already fascinated by toys, especially those that moved. Robots caught my attention the most. I remember wondering how they could move on their own. What was inside them? What made their arms, wheels, or other parts move?

My curiosity eventually became stronger than my fear of breaking things. I started taking apart the robots I had at home just to see what was inside. One day, I found a small metal component inside one of the toys. I learned that it was the motor, the part responsible for making the robot move. I became fascinated by it and immediately wanted to make it work outside the toy.

I tried several times, but nothing happened. I did not understand electricity or circuits at the time. After many unsuccessful attempts, I eventually gave up. I did not realize that this small failure would only be the beginning of a much bigger journey.

Years later, another simple experience brought me back to that curiosity.

During a family trip to the province, my uncles planned to catch frogs at night for food. They brought a light with them so they could see in the darkness. I noticed that they connected wires to the light, and it turned on. Once again, I became curious. I wanted to know how something so simple could produce light.

My uncle encouraged me to try it myself. I connected the wires, and the light turned on. Excited, I tried it repeatedly. However, after a while, it suddenly stopped working. I immediately thought that I had broken it and that I might get scolded. Instead, my uncle showed me what had happened. He stripped the insulation from the wire and connected the exposed metal to the other wire. The light turned on again.

That moment taught me something simple but important: electricity needs a complete path to flow.

Later that night, the light eventually ran out of power. My uncles showed me how they charged the battery using a battery charger. I was amazed that the same object could be used repeatedly by simply restoring its stored energy. What seemed ordinary to them was fascinating to me. I wanted to understand more.

That experience also helped me understand the motor I had discovered years earlier. I realized that I needed to create a closed circuit between the battery and the motor. When I finally connected the positive and negative terminals correctly and saw the motor move, I was incredibly happy. I had solved a problem that I once thought was impossible.

From that point on, electronics became more than a hobby. Whenever I found battery-powered toys, I would take them apart and collect their useful components. Instead of simply throwing the toys away, I used their motors, wires, switches, batteries, and other parts to create things of my own. I made simple flashlights with switches and even small vehicles from cardboard and recycled materials.

Even in Grade 1, I would proudly show my creations to my classmates. Some of them were impressed, while others probably wondered why I enjoyed taking things apart so much. For me, however, every broken toy was an opportunity to learn. I was not interested only in making something work. I wanted to understand why it worked.

As I grew older, my projects became more complicated. I learned that electronics is not always about getting the right answer immediately. Sometimes a circuit will not work. Sometimes a component will fail. Sometimes the problem is something as simple as a loose connection. Instead of being afraid of failure, I learned to troubleshoot, test different possibilities, and find alternative solutions.

This mindset eventually led me to become a technician. What started with a five-year-old child taking apart toys eventually became a serious interest in electronics, repair, and technology. Today, I enjoy building and repairing different kinds of electronic systems, from amplifiers and computers to electrical and solar projects. I also explore microcontrollers such as Arduino and ESP32, where I can combine electronics with programming to create functional projects.

Looking back, I realize that my journey did not begin with a textbook, a laboratory, or an expensive piece of equipment. It began with a child's simple question:“What makes this move?”

That question taught me to be curious. Breaking things taught me to investigate. Failed experiments taught me to keep trying. And finally, solving those problems taught me that I could create something instead of simply using what others had made.

I am still that curious child in many ways. The difference is that I now have more knowledge, more skills, and better tools to pursue the questions that once fascinated me. Electronics has become more than my hobby and more than my profession. It has become a way for me to understand the world, solve problems, and turn ideas into something real.

My journey in electronics began with curiosity, but it continues because I have learned never to be afraid of not knowing the answer. Instead,

“I ask, I test, I fail, I learn, and I try again.”
